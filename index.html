01

What is murrini

Murrini is a picture made of glass that runs all the way through a rod. Cut the rod anywhere and the same picture is on the face of every piece.

The Italian word is murrina (plural murrine); the American boro scene says murrini or just image cane. The idea is ancient — Roman glassmakers were doing it two thousand years ago, and Murano turned it into an industry — but the modern borosilicate version is its own animal: pulled hard, tight and small enough to encase in the wall of a pipe, the face of a marble or the cap of a rig.

A millefiori cane (“a thousand flowers”) is the pattern-based cousin: instead of a picture, the bundle is built from repeating rods so that the face reads as a flower, a honeycomb, a pinwheel or a checkerboard. Pack a bunch of millefiori together and you get the classic Venetian paperweight look. Pack image cane together and you get a comic strip.

Glossary
Cane
Any pulled rod of glass carrying color, pattern or an image through its length.
Murrini / image cane
Cane whose cross-section is a single picture — a face, a logo, a character.
Millie / millefiori
Cane whose cross-section is a repeating pattern built from bundled rods.
Pull
One heating-and-stretching of a bundle. Also what makers call a release (“Pull 305”).
Slice / coin
A cross-section cut off a cane and cold-polished so the image sits under a clean, flat face.
Chip
Thin cuts, partial cuts and off-cuts from the same cane, sold by weight.
COE 33
The expansion coefficient of borosilicate. Every cane here is boro and will play with boro.
02

The process

From a drawing to a coin-sized picture that will never fade, smear or peel — because it isn’t on the glass, it is the glass.

1
Design

The image is reduced to flat color fields with a bold outline. Anything that won’t survive being shrunk tenfold gets cut.

2
Build the bundle

The picture is assembled large from colored rods, pressed sheets and pre-pulled sub-canes, each color laid in like a mosaic. Black outline rod holds the drawing.

3
Encase & heat-soak

Wrapped in clear, attached to punties at both ends, brought up to working heat evenly. Cold spots mean a distorted pull.

4
Pull

Two people walk apart, drawing the molten bundle down to a rod. The image shrinks in proportion but keeps every detail.

5
Anneal & cut

Annealed to relieve stress, then cut into lengths. That’s the cane pieces in this catalog: sold by weight, picture on the end.

6
Slice & cold work

Or the cane goes to the cold shop: sawn into discs, lapped flat and polished so the picture sits under optical glass like a coin.

06

Slices & coins

Cane cut into coins and cold-polished to an optical face — the extra hand that makes a slice cost more than a chip. Everything here was cold-worked by Josh Rose. Cane makers are being identified slice by slice; pricing is coming soon.

How a slice is cold-worked
  1. Saw. Sectioned on a water-cooled diamond saw at roughly 2–4 mm.
  2. Flat lap. Each face ground flat on a diamond lap, starting around 80–120 grit, until both faces are parallel.
  3. Grit progression. 220 → 400 → 600 → 1200, each stage removing the last one’s scratches. Skip a stage and the polish never gets there.
  4. Pre-polish. 3000-grit or a resin pad brings a satin haze.
  5. Polish. Cerium oxide on felt brings the face to full optical clarity.
  6. Clean & inspect. Ultrasonic clean, then inspection under light for scratches, pits and cracks.

How attribution works

Verified means the cane design matches a listing in the maker’s own public catalog — a design attribution, not a chain of ownership. Probable means a strong lead. Attributed means the maker was recorded at intake and not yet matched to a listing. Unattributed means we don’t know yet.

The fine print

All glass is COE 33 borosilicate unless noted. Weights are of the cane pieces on hand at time of indexing; pairs show the pair total. Character and logo canes are artist tributes; no affiliation with any rights holder is implied.
